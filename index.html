Experience digit recognition from the neural network perspective

A small convolutional network, 99% accurate on MNIST, running live in your browser. Weights: ONNX Model Zoo (MIT).

I built these two explainers for my kids and anyone new to neural networks, to show how machines learn and make decisions. Here, you can draw a digit and see how a trained network reaches an answer, including when it gets things wrong.

How does a drawing become a prediction?

Draw a 1, then add strokes to turn it into a 4. Watch the network's answer change.

You recognise the digit almost immediately. This network reaches its answer through layers of simple calculations: starting with pixels, finding patterns, and combining them into a prediction.

Follow your drawing through the network

Read the diagram from the bottom up.

  • You draw: your original handwriting.
  • Network sees: your drawing resized and centred in a 28 × 28 grid. Each pixel becomes a number describing how much ink is there.
  • Layer 1: eight learned filters scan the grid for small visual patterns. The coloured patches show the filters; the squares above show where they respond. Darker areas mean a stronger response.
  • Layer 2: sixteen filters combine patterns from the first layer into more complex ones.
  • Top row: the network assigns a percentage to each digit. The green outline marks its prediction.

Click a filter or a square to inspect it. In the coloured filters, ink in green areas increases the response; ink in magenta areas reduces it.

These scanning filters give a convolutional neural network, or CNN, its name. Each filter is reused across the picture, so the network can look for the same pattern in different places.

Who taught it what a 4 looks like?

Instead of programming rules for every possible handwriting style, we train a network using examples.

This one was trained on tens of thousands of handwritten digits, each labelled with the correct answer. During training, its predictions were compared with those labels. The strengths of its connections, called weights, were adjusted to reduce prediction errors.

This is supervised learning: learning from examples with answers attached.

The model achieves about 99% accuracy on the MNIST test set. Your drawings may be different from those examples, so its accuracy here can vary.

Training happened before this page loaded. Drawing changes its prediction; it does not teach the network anything new.

Can it be confident and wrong?

Draw a letter, a face or a cat.

The network still has only ten answers available: 0–9. It has no "not a digit" option, so it may strongly favour a digit even when your drawing is something else.

The percentages show how strongly it favours each available answer. They do not guarantee that the answer is right.

Next: can a network learn to act?

In the self-driving car demo, you train a small network to drive, three ways. With flashcards, you label road situations with the right move: supervised learning, as here. As a copycat, it learns by watching you drive. By trial and error, nobody gives it any answer: it tries moves and learns from points and penalties.

Here, the feedback is "this drawing is a 4." There, it can also be "that move caused a collision."
